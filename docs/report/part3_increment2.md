## 10. Increment 2 Planning

### 10.1 Sprint Goal

Where the first increment built a repository that could *store* a chain of custody, the second increment built the machinery that makes that chain *provable and rule-bound*. The sprint goal was *"make the chain of custody cryptographically verifiable and procedurally enforced"* — to turn the ordered custody records of Increment 1 from a set of claims into a set of proofs, and to turn the evidence lifecycle from an informal sequence of statuses into a formal automaton that the system enforces. This is the increment in which the first three of the project's five complex algorithms were designed and built, and it was therefore the increment of highest technical risk, which is precisely why it was placed in the middle of the schedule, after the foundation had stabilised.

The increment was budgeted at **thirty-eight story points** across six user stories, listed in Table 10.1. Two of the six — the graph integrity verifier at thirteen points and the two eight-point algorithms — carried the bulk of the increment's weight and its risk; the two report user interfaces were deliberately small, because their value is in exposing the algorithms' output rather than in themselves.

### 10.2 User Stories and Acceptance Criteria

**US-12 — Ed25519-signed custody events (5 points).** *As a lead investigator, I want each custody event digitally signed by the outgoing holder, so that records cannot be forged or repudiated.* The story is satisfied when a canonical payload — the evidence identifier, the event type, the timestamp, the two investigators, and the file hash — is signed at every upload and transfer, when the signatures verify against the signer's stored public key, and when the signature column is non-nullable so that an unsigned custody record cannot exist.

**US-13 — Graph integrity verification (13 points).** *As an analyst, I want the entire custody history verified, not just the latest record, so that tampering anywhere in the chain is detected.* The story is satisfied when the custody events are built into a graph and traversed by depth-first search from the upload root; when each node is subjected to three checks — hash consistency, signature validity, and strictly increasing timestamps; when all failures are collected in a single pass rather than stopping at the first; and when the output is a structured report giving a verdict of Intact, Partially Verified, or Broken, a completeness score, an ordered list of failure points, and a verified timeline.

**US-14 — Lifecycle state machine (8 points).** *As a lead investigator, I want evidence to move only through legally meaningful states, so that procedural rules are enforced by the system.* The story is satisfied when the states and their permitted transitions are defined in a transition table; when each transition carries a minimum role and a set of preconditions; when rejected transitions return the exact unmet condition and are audited; and when every successful transition requires a justification and triggers a recalculation of the item's risk score.

**US-15 — Custody chain reconstruction (8 points).** *As an analyst, I want plausible reconstruction of gapped custody chains, so that partial records still support the investigation honestly.* The story is satisfied when gaps are detected between confirmed records; when candidate filling records are generated from the access log and scored on multiple criteria; when a best-first search selects the most plausible candidate above a threshold, marking the gap unresolved otherwise; and when every link is labelled Confirmed, Inferred, or Unresolved, with an overall confidence score, and inferred records are never presented as confirmed.

**US-16 and US-17 — the report user interfaces (2 points each).** *As an analyst, I want readable pages for the verification and reconstruction results, so that I can interpret the algorithms' output quickly.* These stories are satisfied when the integrity report page shows the verdict, the completeness percentage, and the failure table, with a JSON endpoint for programmatic access, and when the reconstruction page shows the full timeline with Confirmed, Inferred, and Unresolved links visually distinguished and each inferred link's reasoning displayed.

## 11. Complex Algorithm Design

This chapter is the analytical heart of the increment and of the report's demonstration of Category C work. It gives an algorithmic account of the four pieces of complex logic delivered here — the signing that underpins them, and the three algorithms proper — with enough detail to make their complexity demonstrable rather than merely asserted.

### 11.1 Digital Signing of Custody Events (US-12)

Signing is the primitive on which the graph integrity verifier depends, and although the Ed25519 algorithm itself is a Category B library call, the design decisions around it are the project's own. The central decision, taken as the Increment 1 retrospective had resolved it should be, was to fix the **canonical payload** before writing any signing code. The payload that is signed for a custody event is a JSON serialisation, with sorted keys, of exactly six fields: the evidence identifier, the event type, the timestamp in ISO format, the outgoing and incoming investigator identifiers, and the file hash at the event. Sorting the keys makes the serialisation deterministic, so that the bytes produced when the record is created are identical to the bytes reconstructed when it is later verified — a subtle requirement whose violation is exactly the kind of defect that a signer and a verifier written independently tend to exhibit, and which fixing the format up front prevented.

When a custody event is created, the outgoing investigator's private key — decrypted transparently from its Fernet-encrypted form at rest — signs this payload, and the resulting signature is stored on the custody record. Because every investigator account is issued an Ed25519 key pair when it is created, signing succeeds in normal operation and every custody event carries a genuine signature. The signature column is non-nullable, which encodes a deliberate policy: a custody record must always carry a signature slot. The error-handling behaviour around this policy, which the maintenance work refined, is deliberately **fail-visible** rather than fail-silent: if signing is ever unavailable — an account somehow without a key, or a cryptographic error — the system records an explicit unsigned sentinel and warns the user, and the graph integrity verifier then reports that link as unverified rather than accepting it as valid. This behaviour was chosen over two worse alternatives. It is better than the earlier behaviour, which left the non-nullable column unset and raised a database error that aborted the transfer, and it is better than silently storing a placeholder that would look signed; instead, an unsigned event is made conspicuous both in the interface, through the warning, and in the integrity report, through a verification failure at that node. For a forensic system, making an unverifiable custody event impossible to overlook is the defensible position, and the report describes this behaviour as it is implemented rather than claiming a stronger fail-closed guarantee than the code provides.

### 11.2 Chain-of-Custody Graph Integrity Verification (US-13)

**Purpose and why it is complex.** The verifier's purpose is to prove, or disprove, the integrity of an evidence item's *entire* custody history rather than merely its most recent record. This distinction is the whole point: a system that checks only the latest custody entry can be defeated by tampering with any earlier one, and legal admissibility depends on the whole chain, not its tail. Verifying the whole chain cannot be expressed as a database query or a single library call, because it requires modelling the history as a connected structure, walking it, and applying several independent cryptographic and temporal checks at every step while accumulating a complete picture of where — if anywhere — it fails. This is the reasoning behind its thirteen-point estimate, the largest single estimate in the project.

**Data structure.** The algorithm loads all custody-log records for the item, ordered by timestamp, and constructs a directed acyclic graph in memory. Each node wraps one custody record and holds a list of outgoing edges to its successors. In the common case the chain is linear — each event has a single successor — but the structure is a general graph so that a future branching scenario, in which an item is split, could be represented without redesign. The root of the graph is the Upload event, the one custody record that has no predecessor.

**Traversal and per-node validation.** The graph is traversed by depth-first search from the root. At each node the algorithm applies three checks in sequence. The **hash check** has two parts. On the root Upload node it performs the ground-truth verification: it decrypts the stored evidence file, recomputes its SHA-256, and compares the result with the item's immutable original hash, thereby detecting whether the physical file on disk has been altered since intake. On every node — and this is a point of honesty in the design — it performs a recorded-hash consistency check: each custody record stores the file hash as it was at that event, and because the system stores a single evidence blob rather than a distinct historical snapshot per event, every node's recorded hash must equal the immutable original; a node whose recorded hash is missing or divergent indicates either that the file was swapped at that point or that the custody record was edited after signing, both of which are integrity failures. The report is explicit that this is a consistency check of recorded hashes against a ground truth rather than a re-hash of per-event file snapshots that the system does not store — a deliberate choice to describe what the code actually does rather than to claim a stronger guarantee than the data model supports. The **signature check** reconstructs the canonical payload for the node and verifies its stored Ed25519 signature against the signing investigator's public key, so that a record altered after signing is detected. The **timestamp check** confirms that the node's timestamp is strictly greater than its predecessor's, so that a duplicate or backdated record is detected.

**Single-pass failure collection.** A crucial design property, and the one that the Increment 2 retrospective records as the source of a three-day mid-sprint plateau, is that the traversal does *not* stop at the first failure. When a check fails, the algorithm records a structured failure — the node's identifier, event type, timestamp, the failure category, and a human-readable explanation — and continues the traversal, so that all failures across the entire chain are discovered in a single pass. The first implementation stopped at the first failure, which contradicted the acceptance criterion that all failures be found; reworking the traversal to accumulate failures while continuing is what the plateau on the burndown chart represents, and it also surfaced the payload-format bug that fixing the canonical format up front had been meant to prevent but that a subtle timestamp-formatting mismatch had reintroduced.

**Output.** After the traversal, the algorithm computes a completeness score as the percentage of nodes that passed all three checks, and it assigns a verdict: *Intact* if there were no failures at all, *Partially Verified* if some nodes passed but others failed, and *Broken* if no node could be verified or there were no records to verify. The result is a structured report carrying the verdict, the completeness score, the counts of total and verified nodes, the ordered list of failure points, and a verified timeline, and it is serialisable to a dictionary so that it can be stored as an audit record and consumed by the risk scorer. The sequence diagram for this algorithm shows exactly this flow — the route invoking the engine, the engine loading the records and walking the graph, and the per-node checks against the database — and it is the reference for the implementation.

**Complexity.** The traversal visits each of the *n* custody nodes once and performs a constant number of checks at each, so the time complexity is linear in the number of custody records, dominated in practice by the cryptographic operations (one signature verification and, at the root, one file decryption and hash) rather than by the graph walk itself. This is an appropriate cost for an operation that is run on demand rather than on every request.

### 11.3 Evidence Lifecycle State Machine (US-14)

**Purpose.** The state machine turns the evidence lifecycle from a convention that investigators are trained to follow into a rule that the system enforces. Its purpose is to guarantee that no item can be moved into an invalid state, that only authorised roles can trigger particular transitions, and that some transitions cannot occur until their preconditions are met — for example, that an item cannot be archived until its integrity has been verified as intact.

**The automaton.** The lifecycle comprises six states — Collected, Submitted, Under Analysis, Transferred, Archived, and Flagged — and the permitted transitions among them are defined declaratively in a transition table that maps each ordered pair of states to the minimum roles permitted to make that transition and a list of preconditions that must hold. The normal progression runs Collected to Submitted to Under Analysis, with Under Analysis and Transferred able to move to each other so that an item can be handed off and taken back during analysis, and terminates in Archived. Separately, any active state can transition to Flagged, the state an item enters when an integrity problem is detected, and only an administrator can move an item out of Flagged back into Under Analysis after resolving the issue. Representing the lifecycle as a table rather than as branching code was a deliberate choice: it makes the whole policy visible and auditable in one place, it makes adding or changing a transition a data change rather than a control-flow change, and it is exactly the structure the state diagram depicts.

**The validation pipeline.** A transition request runs through a fixed pipeline. First, the algorithm checks that the requested ordered pair of states exists in the transition table; a pair that is absent is an invalid transition and is rejected. Second, it checks that the requesting investigator's role is among those permitted for that transition. Third, it evaluates every precondition attached to the transition — that at least one custody log entry exists before analysis may begin, or that an integrity check with an Intact verdict exists before archiving — each expressed as a small predicate function. Only if all three stages pass is the transition executed: the item's state is updated, the change is written to the audit trail together with the triggering investigator, the timestamp, and a mandatory justification, and the item's risk score is recalculated to reflect the new state. If any stage fails, the transition is rejected with a message identifying exactly which condition was not met, and the rejection is itself written to the audit trail, so that failed attempts are as fully recorded as successful ones.

**Why it is complex.** The state machine is more than a field update guarded by an `if` statement. It is a formal automaton whose transition relation, role guards, and precondition predicates together encode a body of procedural law, and whose execution has side effects — auditing and risk recalculation — that must occur atomically with the state change. Its correctness is what allows the rest of the system to trust that an Archived item really did pass integrity verification, a guarantee that the archiving precondition enforces by checking for an Intact integrity-check audit record before permitting the transition.

### 11.4 Custody Chain Reconstruction (US-15)

**Purpose and why it is complex.** Reconstruction addresses the situation in which the custody record is incomplete — a transfer was never recorded, an entry was deleted, or timestamps are inconsistent — and the investigator nonetheless needs the most plausible account of what happened, clearly separated into what is certain, what is inferred, and what cannot be resolved. This is fundamentally a heuristic search problem rather than a lookup: there is no single correct answer when records are missing, so the algorithm must generate possible filling records, evaluate their plausibility against several criteria, and return the best supported reconstruction together with an honest measure of confidence. That is why it is Category C work and why it carries an eight-point estimate.

**Gap detection.** The algorithm first loads the confirmed custody records in timestamp order and walks each consecutive pair, deciding whether a gap lies between them. A gap is detected in either of two ways. A **structural gap** exists when the holder implied by one record does not match the holder named by the next — for instance, when investigator B is recorded as receiving an item but there is no record of A, its previous holder, transferring it. A **temporal gap** exists when more than a configured number of hours — seventy-two by default — separates two consecutive records with no intervening transfer, on the principle that an item that sat unaccounted-for for days is a chain-of-custody concern even if the holder fields happen to align. The temporal criterion was added during the increment's development because structural matching alone missed the common real-world case of an item that is simply unattended for an extended period.

**Candidate generation and scoring.** For each detected gap, the algorithm generates candidate filling records by querying the access log for events that fall within the gap's time window and involve investigators who could plausibly have handled the item. Each candidate is then scored by a plausibility function that combines four independent criteria: **temporal proximity**, how close the candidate's timestamp is to the expected transfer window; **role consistency**, whether the candidate investigator's role is permitted to hold evidence of this item's category, evaluated against an explicit role-to-category permission matrix; **location consistency**, whether the candidate's recorded location is plausible given the locations of the surrounding confirmed records; and **behavioural consistency**, whether the candidate investigator had other interactions with the same case around that time. The combined score lies between zero and one, and the algorithm retains the breakdown across the four criteria so that an inferred link can explain itself.

**Best-first selection and labelling.** For each gap the candidates are sorted by score and the highest is selected. If its score meets or exceeds the plausibility threshold — set at 0.40 — the gap is filled with an *Inferred* link carrying that candidate's details, its confidence, and a generated explanation quoting the four sub-scores. If no candidate reaches the threshold, the gap is left as an *Unresolved* link that records the time window it spans and states plainly that no candidate was plausible enough. The confirmed records themselves are labelled *Confirmed* with full confidence. This three-way labelling is the algorithm's central honesty guarantee: an inferred record is visibly distinguished from a confirmed one and is never presented as fact, which directly addresses the risk, identified in the project proposal, that a reconstruction might mislead an investigator into treating an estimate as certainty.

**Output.** The algorithm assembles the reconstructed chain as an ordered list of these labelled links and computes an overall confidence score as the proportion of expected links that are either confirmed or inferred, so that a chain with many unresolved gaps scores low and a fully confirmed chain scores one hundred per cent. The activity diagram for this algorithm depicts exactly this flow, from loading the confirmed records through gap detection, candidate generation, scoring, best-first selection, and labelling, and it is the reference for the implementation. As with the verifier, the traversal is linear in the number of confirmed records, with the per-gap candidate scoring bounded by the number of access-log events in each gap window.

## 12. Implementation and User Interface

The three algorithms were implemented as self-contained engine classes in the business-logic layer, each constructed with an evidence item and exposing a single public method — `verify`, `transition_to`, and `reconstruct` respectively — that returns a structured result object. Keeping each algorithm behind a single method with a structured return, and out of the route handlers entirely, is what made them unit-testable in isolation and is the reason the increment's substantial test coverage was achievable. The route layer's job in this increment is thin: the custody blueprint exposes routes that construct the relevant engine, invoke its method, persist the result to the audit trail where appropriate, and hand the structured result to a template.

The two report user interfaces (US-16 and US-17) render those structured results. The integrity report page presents the verdict as a coloured banner, the completeness score as a percentage, and the failures as a table locating each by node, type, and reason, with a companion JSON endpoint returning the same report for programmatic use. The reconstruction report page renders the reconstructed chain as a timeline in which Confirmed, Inferred, and Unresolved links are visually distinct — a colour and a label per category — and each inferred link displays its confidence and the reasoning behind it, so that the human reader can see not just what the algorithm concluded but why. The signing of custody events (US-12) was woven into the existing custody-transfer route and the upload route from Increment 1, so that from this increment onward every custody event is signed as it is created.

## 13. Testing

Increment 2 was validated by the state-machine, graph-integrity, and chain-reconstruction test classes in the increment's test module, together with the cryptographic signing and verification tests. The state-machine tests assert that valid transitions succeed and invalid ones are rejected, that the wrong role is refused, that the custody-log precondition blocks analysis when no custody entry exists, that any state can be flagged, that a Read-Only investigator has no available transitions, that flagging raises the risk level, and that rejected transitions are written to the audit trail. The graph-integrity tests assert that a clean chain returns Intact, that a missing file is detected as a hash mismatch, that a non-increasing timestamp is detected as an anomaly, that an evidence item with no custody records returns Broken, and that the report serialises correctly. The reconstruction tests assert that a gapless chain returns full confidence, that a holder mismatch produces a detected gap that is either inferred or marked unresolved, and that an empty chain returns zero confidence. These tests are example-based and each constructs the minimal records needed to drive one behaviour, reusing the fixtures established in Increment 1. Building this coverage was made much cheaper by the design decision to keep each algorithm behind a single method on an engine object: a test can construct the engine and assert on its structured result without any HTTP machinery.

## 14. Sprint Review, Burndown and Retrospective

### 14.1 Sprint Review

At the review, all six stories were demonstrated against their acceptance criteria and all thirty-eight points were accepted. The increment delivered signed custody events, a graph verifier that walks an entire chain and reports a verdict with a completeness score and located failures, a state machine that enforces the lifecycle with role guards and preconditions, and a reconstruction algorithm that fills gaps honestly with confidence labelling. Against the five guarantees of the problem statement, Increment 2 completed the *provable integrity* guarantee begun in Increment 1 and delivered the *enforced procedural rules* guarantee in full, leaving only analytical prioritisation for Increment 3.

### 14.2 Burndown

The burndown chart (`diagrams/increment2_agile_burndown_chart.svg`) shows the roughest middle of the project. The actual line tracks the ideal for the first several days, then plateaus across days five to seven, during which almost no points were burned. The plateau is the graph-integrity rework described in Section 11.2: the depth-first traversal, initially written to stop at the first failure, had to be reworked to accumulate all failures while continuing, and that rework surfaced a payload-format mismatch between the signer and the verifier that took further time to diagnose. From about day eight the line resumes its descent and steepens, because the state machine and the reconstruction algorithm were able to reuse the test fixtures and the graph-building patterns established while building the verifier, and all thirty-eight points were delivered by day fourteen. The chart's annotation records the plateau and its cause.

### 14.3 Retrospective

The retrospective concluded that the day-zero design spike carried in from Increment 1 had paid off directly — freezing the canonical payload format before writing signing code is what made the eventual payload-mismatch bug tractable rather than mysterious — and that building reusable test fixtures while constructing the first algorithm sharply reduced the cost of testing the second and third. What went less well was the three-day plateau, and its lesson was estimation-related: the thirteen-point estimate for the verifier had been correct in magnitude but wrong in its reasoning, because the cryptographic checks that had seemed hard turned out to be straightforward library calls while the traversal semantics that had seemed straightforward turned out to be the real difficulty. The concrete actions carried into Increment 3 were two: to write the acceptance criteria of each complex algorithm as unit tests *before* implementing it, so that the definition of "all failures found" or "score in range" was pinned down in advance, and to keep the language model behind an interface so that the assistant pipeline could be tested without external credits. Both actions, as the next chapter records, were followed and both paid off.
