## 15. Increment 3 Planning

### 15.1 Sprint Goal

The first two increments produced a system that stored a chain of custody, proved its integrity, and enforced its lifecycle. The third increment turns that trustworthy record into something an investigator can *act on efficiently*, and hardens the whole system to the standard a forensic application demands. The sprint goal was *"turn verified records into judgement, and make the system safe to deploy"* — to deliver the analytical layer (risk scoring, anomaly detection, and the AI assistant) that helps an investigator prioritise among many items, the presentation layer (dashboards and reports) that surfaces that analysis, and the security hardening (two-factor authentication, encryption at rest, content validation, and tamper-evident export) that a system holding evidence cannot do without.

This was the largest increment, budgeted at **forty-two story points** across ten user stories, listed in Table 15.1. It contains two of the project's five complex algorithms — the multi-factor risk scorer and the AI assistant's orchestration workflow — alongside a cluster of third-party integrations and security features. It was placed last deliberately: the risk scorer depends on the integrity verifier and the state machine built in Increment 2, and the AI assistant's most valuable capability, answering integrity questions, depends on being able to run a fresh verification.

### 15.2 User Stories and Acceptance Criteria

**US-18 — Multi-factor risk scoring (8 points).** *As a lead investigator, I want each item scored across multiple interacting signals, so that I can prioritise attention without reading every log.* Satisfied when eight factors are extracted and each normalised with a curve suited to its distribution; when they are combined by a weighted sum amplified by interaction rules; when the final score in the range zero to one is classified into a level and accompanied by per-factor explanations and generated recommendations; and when items are ranked highest-risk first.

**US-19 — Configurable weights and thresholds (3 points).** *As an administrator, I want to tune the factor weights and classification thresholds, so that scoring reflects our unit's priorities.* Satisfied when an admin-only settings page allows the weights and thresholds to be edited, when the weights are re-normalised to sum to one, when the thresholds must be strictly increasing, when the settings persist across restarts, and when changes are audited.

**US-20 — AI assistant orchestration (8 points).** *As an investigator, I want to ask questions in plain English and get answers grounded in the data I am allowed to see, so that I can query the system conversationally.* Satisfied when a multi-stage pipeline classifies the question's intent, retrieves the relevant database context scoped to the user's accessible cases, constructs a prompt, calls the language model, and validates the response, degrading gracefully to a context-aware mock when no API key is configured.

**US-21 — Hallucination detection (3 points).** *As an investigator, I want the system to flag answers that reference entities not in the retrieved data, so that I never act on invented information.* Satisfied when every case number, evidence identifier, and investigator name in the model's reply is cross-checked against the retrieved context, and unknown references produce a visible warning.

**US-22 — Isolation Forest anomaly detection (5 points).** *As a lead investigator, I want statistical outliers in custody activity flagged, so that unusual behaviour is suggested for review.* Satisfied when a six-dimensional feature vector is built for each item, when an Isolation Forest flags outliers, and when the results are presented as "suggested for review" alongside the deterministic risk score.

**US-23 — Risk and anomaly dashboards (3 points).** *As a lead investigator, I want dashboards ranking items by risk and anomaly status, so that the highest-priority evidence is one click away.* Satisfied when a risk-ranked table, an anomaly view, and a per-item risk detail page present the analysis clearly.

**US-24, US-25, US-26, US-27 — hardening and reporting.** These stories add two-factor authentication with lockout and idle timeout; AES-256-GCM encryption of stored files with magic-byte content validation and encrypted private keys; a tamper-evident signed export package; and a printable per-item PDF custody report. Their acceptance criteria are given where each is implemented in Chapter 17.

## 16. Complex Algorithm and Workflow Design

### 16.1 Multi-Factor Evidence Risk Scoring (US-18)

**Purpose and why it is complex.** The risk scorer's purpose is to reduce the many signals that bear on how much an evidence item should worry an investigator — how many times it has changed hands, whether its integrity has failed, how long it has sat untouched, how many different people have handled it — into a single comparable number, so that hundreds of items can be ranked and the most concerning surfaced first. A naive rule such as "flag items with more than five transfers" is inadequate because it ignores the *interaction* between signals: an item with many transfers, a failed integrity check, and several handlers is far more concerning than an item with many transfers alone. Capturing those interactions in a defensible, tunable, explainable score is what makes this Category C work rather than a formula.

**The eight factors.** For each item the algorithm extracts eight signals. Six of these correspond to the signals identified in the project proposal — the number of custody transfers, the maximum time-gap between consecutive transfers, the number of distinct investigators who have handled the item, the number of failed integrity checks, the number of role-permission mismatches, and the current lifecycle state encoded as an ordinal risk value — and two further factors, the inherent risk of the file's type and the number of duplicate custody entries, round out the picture. Two of these factors deserve particular note because of a design decision taken during the maintenance work: the role-mismatch and duplicate-entry counts are computed *live* by the Category A audit-formula checks built in Increment 1, so that the simple formula computations feed directly into the complex scoring algorithm rather than sitting in isolation, and the integrity factor reads the item's integrity status as written back by the graph verifier, so that the highest-weighted signal reflects a genuine verification result rather than a constant.

**Normalisation.** Each raw signal is normalised to the range zero to one using a curve matched to its natural distribution, because the signals are not comparable in their raw units. A categorical signal such as integrity status is mapped directly — verified to zero, not-checked to one-half, failed to one. Count-like signals with heavy tails, such as the number of transfers or distinct handlers, use a logarithmic curve so that the difference between one and three handlers matters more than the difference between eleven and thirteen. The time-since-last-activity signal uses a sigmoid centred at thirty days, so that risk rises sharply as an item passes the point where prolonged inactivity becomes suspicious rather than rising linearly from day one. Choosing a curve per factor, rather than normalising everything linearly, is a small piece of modelling that materially improves the score's behaviour and is one of the algorithm's non-trivial design choices.

**Weighting and interaction.** The normalised factors are combined by a weighted sum. The default weights — integrity status at 0.25, the transfer frequency at 0.13, the file type and role mismatch each at 0.12, the distinct-handler, duplicate, and lifecycle factors each at 0.10, and inactivity at 0.08 — sum to one and reflect the relative importance of each signal, with integrity failure weighted highest because it is the most direct evidence of a problem. On top of this base score the algorithm applies **interaction detection**: five rules each name a pair of factors and thresholds, and when both factors in a rule exceed their thresholds a bonus is added, computed so that the interaction contributes more when the base risk is already elevated. This is the mechanism that makes the combination of, say, an integrity failure and a role mismatch score higher than either alone would, capturing the intuition that co-occurring problems are more than the sum of their parts.

**Classification and explanation.** The final score, clamped to one, is classified into one of four levels — Low, Medium, High, or Critical — using thresholds that default to 0.25, 0.50, and 0.75 but are, like the weights, configurable. The algorithm then generates a plain-language explanation naming the factors that contributed most and a set of recommendations keyed to which factors are elevated, so that the output is not an opaque number but an account an investigator can read and act on. The activity diagram depicts this whole flow — the parallel extraction and normalisation of the eight factors, the weighted sum, the iteration over the five interaction rules, the classification against the thresholds, and the recommendation stage — and it is the reference for the implementation. The score is recomputed whenever an item's state changes, because the lifecycle-state factor and the integrity factor are both inputs, which is why the state machine of Increment 2 triggers a risk recalculation on every transition.

### 16.2 Admin-Configurable Weights and Thresholds (US-19)

The tunability promised in US-18 is realised by US-19 as an administration page on which the eight weights and the three thresholds can be edited. The design makes two guarantees that keep the configuration well-formed. The weights the administrator enters are **re-normalised to sum to one** before use, so that an administrator can express relative importance in any convenient numbers without having to make them add up. The thresholds are validated to be **strictly increasing**, so that the Low, Medium, High, and Critical bands cannot overlap or invert. The settings are persisted to a file and reloaded at startup, so they survive a restart, and every change is written to the audit trail, so that a shift in how risk is measured is itself part of the record. This story is a hybrid of Categories C and A: the re-normalisation and validation logic is the developer's own, while the persistence is straightforward data handling.

### 16.3 AI Investigation Assistant — Orchestration Workflow (US-20)

**Purpose and why it is complex.** The assistant lets an investigator ask questions in natural language — "what is the risk score for evidence seven?", "who has held this item?", "what are my cases?" — and receive answers grounded in the actual database rather than in the language model's imagination. The language model itself is a Category B service; what the project contributes, and what makes this Category C, is the orchestration workflow around it: the logic that decides what the question is about, retrieves exactly the right data while respecting the user's access boundaries, constructs a prompt, and — critically — validates and fact-checks the model's answer before showing it. The value and the difficulty are entirely in this surrounding pipeline.

**The six stages.** The pipeline runs in six stages, depicted in the sequence diagram. First, **intent classification** analyses the question with a keyword-frequency classifier that assigns it to one of several categories — a case query, an evidence or risk or integrity query, a custody query, a general-forensics question, or, checked with priority, an *identity* query such as "who am I" or "my cases" — and extracts any entity identifier the question mentions. Second, **context retrieval** runs a targeted database query determined by the intent and, importantly, **scoped to the cases the current investigator may access**, so that the assistant can never retrieve or reveal data outside the user's remit; the retrieved records are serialised into a compact context block. Third, **prompt construction** assembles a system prompt that embeds this context and states who the current user is, so that identity questions are answered from the authenticated user rather than guessed, and appends the recent conversation history. Fourth, **the model call** sends the prompt to the NVIDIA NIM endpoint hosting a Llama 3.1 model, with a request timeout so that a slow response cannot hang the interface, and falls back to a context-aware mock reply when no API key is configured, which is what allows the entire pipeline to be tested without external credits. Fifth, **response validation** checks the reply for minimum length, for refusal patterns, and for off-topic drift. Sixth, **hallucination detection**, described next, cross-checks the reply against the retrieved data.

**Design decisions.** Two decisions shape this pipeline. The **access scoping in stage two** is not an afterthought but the property that makes the assistant safe: because retrieval is filtered to the user's accessible cases, every downstream stage operates only on permitted data, so the assistant inherits the same case-level authorisation as the rest of the system rather than becoming a hole in it. The **mock fallback in stage four** is what made the pipeline testable and is an example of the "dependency behind a seam" practice carried in from the Increment 2 retrospective: the model is invoked through a single method that the tests can exercise without a network call, so the classification, retrieval, prompt construction, validation, and hallucination stages are all covered by fast, deterministic tests.

### 16.4 Hallucination Detection (US-21)

Hallucination detection is the sixth stage of the pipeline and the project's principal mitigation for the fundamental limitation of language models: they can produce fluent, plausible text that is simply false. The check extracts every entity reference from the model's reply — case numbers, evidence identifiers, and investigator names — and cross-checks each against the context that was actually retrieved and injected into the prompt. A reference that does not appear in the retrieved context is flagged as a potential fabrication, and the flags are surfaced to the user as an explicit warning attached to the answer. Because the retrieval in stage two is access-scoped, this check does double duty: it catches both invented entities and any attempt by the model to mention a real entity that the user was not permitted to see, since neither will be present in the scoped context. The check does not, and cannot, guarantee that the model's prose is correct — it verifies references, not reasoning — and the report is candid that the assistant is positioned as a convenience whose output is to be verified rather than as an authority. But it directly addresses the most dangerous failure mode, an answer that confidently names an evidence item or investigator that does not exist or that the user cannot access.

### 16.5 Isolation Forest Anomaly Detection (US-22)

The anomaly-detection pipeline is a Category B feature — the Isolation Forest is scikit-learn's algorithm, not the project's — but the feature engineering around it is the project's own. For each evidence item the pipeline builds a six-dimensional feature vector: the transfer count, the transfer rate, the number of unique handlers, the maximum gap in days between custody events, an integrity flag, and the file-type risk. It fits an Isolation Forest across all the items in scope and maps each item's anomaly score into the range zero to one, flagging as anomalous those above a threshold. The results are deliberately framed as "suggested for review" rather than as confirmed findings, and they are presented alongside the deterministic risk score rather than in place of it; on the risk dashboard the two are combined into a unified score that weights the deterministic risk at 0.6 and the anomaly score at 0.4, so that the statistical outlier detection informs prioritisation without overriding the explainable, rule-based scoring. This framing is a direct response to the limitation, acknowledged in Part I, that an unsupervised model trained on a small dataset can produce false positives: the design ensures that a human always sees the anomaly flag next to a score they can actually reason about.

## 17. Implementation

### 17.1 Dashboards and Reporting Views (US-23)

The risk dashboard scores every accessible item, combines each item's deterministic and anomaly scores into the unified score, and presents the items ranked highest-risk first, with the per-item risk detail page giving the full factor breakdown, the interaction effects, and the generated recommendations. The anomaly dashboard runs the Isolation Forest pipeline and lists the items by anomaly score with their feature vectors. The main dashboard aggregates the analysis into an at-a-glance view with charts for the risk-level distribution, the lifecycle-state distribution, the audit-event mix, and recent security activity. It is worth stating plainly, for the honesty of the increment narrative, that the dashboards as delivered in this increment presented these aggregates across all data; the work of scoping every one of them to the viewing investigator's case memberships and tailoring the panels per role was a defect fix and enhancement carried out in the subsequent maintenance iteration, and it is documented as such in Part V rather than backdated into this increment.

### 17.2 Two-Factor Authentication, Lockout and Idle Timeout (US-24)

The account-security story hardens authentication in three ways. Time-based one-time-password two-factor authentication is offered through PyOTP, with a QR-code enrolment so that an investigator can register the account with an authenticator application; when it is enabled, a correct password is only the first factor and the login is completed only after a valid code is entered. Repeated failed logins lock an account for a configured period, with an administrator able to unlock it, so that password guessing is throttled. The login route is additionally rate-limited. Together with the sliding idle-session timeout built in Increment 1, these controls mean that a stolen password alone does not grant lasting access.

### 17.3 Encryption at Rest and Content Validation (US-25)

The data-protection story ensures that neither a stolen disk nor a disguised file can do harm. Evidence files are encrypted at rest with AES-256-GCM, with the hashes computed on the plaintext at intake so that integrity verification still works against the decrypted content; the graph verifier decrypts transparently before re-hashing. Uploaded files are validated by their magic bytes so that a file's true content type is checked against its declared extension, and executables are rejected outright, defending against a renamed executable masquerading as an image. Investigators' private keys are themselves encrypted at rest with a Fernet key derived from the application secret, so that a database compromise alone does not yield usable signing keys.

### 17.4 Tamper-Evident Export and PDF Report (US-26, US-27)

The two reporting-out stories produce court-ready artefacts. The case export assembles a ZIP package containing the decrypted evidence files, a manifest recording each file's SHA-256, the full audit trail for the case, and an Ed25519 signature of the manifest together with the instructions and public key needed to verify it, so that the recipient can confirm the package has not been altered since export. The per-item PDF report, generated with ReportLab, produces a printable chain-of-custody document containing the item's identification, a cryptographic integrity summary that embeds a *fresh* run of the graph verifier so that the document reflects the item's current verified state, the full custody table, and a generation footer. Both are Category B features in their serialisation but depend on the Category C integrity work to make their contents meaningful.

## 18. Testing

Increment 3 is the most heavily tested increment, and its test module is correspondingly the largest, reflecting the retrospective action carried from Increment 2 to write the algorithms' acceptance criteria as tests before implementing them. The risk-scoring tests exercise the normalisation curves directly — including the edge cases that test-first thinking surfaced, such as equal normalisation bounds and negative inputs to the logarithmic curve — and assert that the weights sum to one, that a report has the expected eight factors, that the final score stays in range, that classification lands in the right band, and that interaction bonuses fire only when both thresholds are met. The AI-assistant tests cover each stage of the pipeline independently: intent classification across every category and entity-extraction case, context retrieval, prompt construction including history trimming, response validation including refusal detection, and the full pipeline end to end against the mock backend. The anomaly tests cover the feature engineering and the pipeline's output shape and ordering. The route tests confirm that the dashboards and the assistant endpoints require login and behave correctly. This increment's tests, together with the dedicated intent-classifier module added later, are the bulk of the project's suite, and the fact that the entire assistant pipeline is testable without a live model — because of the mock seam — is what made that depth of coverage practical.

## 19. Sprint Review, Burndown and Retrospective

### 19.1 Sprint Review

At the review, all ten stories were demonstrated and all forty-two points accepted. The increment delivered the multi-factor risk scorer with configurable weights, the six-stage AI assistant with hallucination detection, the Isolation Forest anomaly pipeline, the dashboards, the full security-hardening suite, and the signed export and PDF report. With this increment the fifth of the problem statement's guarantees — analytical prioritisation — was delivered, completing the set, and the system reached the functional scope defined at the outset.

### 19.2 Burndown

The burndown chart (Figure 19.1) shows the steadiest of the three increments, tracking within about two points of the ideal throughout, with a slight early slip on days one to three that was recovered by day four. The steadiness is directly attributable to the two retrospective actions carried in from Increment 2: writing the risk-scoring tests before the implementation caught the normalisation edge cases before they could reach a route and cause a late surprise, and hiding the language model behind a mock seam meant the assistant pipeline could be built and tested continuously rather than being blocked on external credits or network availability. The security stories integrated cleanly because the audit and custody layers from the earlier increments already exposed the hooks they needed.

### 19.3 Retrospective

The retrospective judged the two carried-in actions to have paid off measurably, and recorded a general principle worth carrying forward: the parts of the system that were given the most tests and the most isolation — the algorithms and the external model — were the parts that caused the fewest surprises, while the one significant defect class that emerged did so in exactly the area that had *not* been given a dedicated test focus. That defect class was access scoping in the aggregate views and the assistant: the dashboards and the assistant's retrieval, built in this increment, aggregated across all data, and it was only during deliberate review testing — the tester persona actively trying to see another case's data — that the leakage was found. This finding did not fit within the increment and was not patched over; it was taken up as a formal maintenance iteration, described in Part V, which is itself the increment narrative's closing lesson: an honest agile process surfaces the defects its own testing reveals and schedules the work to fix them rather than quietly absorbing them.
