# Part V — Maintenance Iterations: Access-Scoping (M1) and Correctness & Hardening (M2)

Two maintenance iterations followed the three delivery increments. The first, M1, resolved two access-scoping defects found during review testing. The second, M2, resolved a set of correctness and security defects found during a structured code-review pass. Both are documented here as first-class iterations, because the way a project handles change and defect repair after its planned scope is complete is as much a part of the software-engineering story as the original construction.

---

## 20. Maintenance Iteration M1 — Access-Scoping Change Requests

### 20.1 Change After Delivery in the Incremental Model

The three increments delivered the full functional scope defined at the project's outset, and at the end of Increment 3 the system met all of its acceptance criteria and passed its test suite. In a plan-driven project that would have been the end of the engineering narrative. In an incremental, agile project it is not, because one of the model's defining strengths is that it accommodates change discovered *after* a working version exists without treating that change as a failure of planning. This part documents a maintenance iteration, labelled M1, that followed the three increments and resolved two defects found during review testing. It is presented as a first-class iteration, with the same planning-and-reflection structure as the increments, for two reasons. First, handling change after delivery is a core software-engineering activity that a report demonstrating the discipline should show rather than hide. Second, the specific defects M1 resolved are instructive: they were not crashes or failing tests — the system worked and its tests were green — but *authorisation* defects, the most dangerous kind for a forensic system and precisely the kind that ordinary functional testing does not catch. Documenting how they were found, understood, and fixed is more valuable to the report than a pretence that the delivered increments were flawless.

The iteration was scoped at **ten story points** across two change-request stories, US-28 and US-29, each addressing one of the two defects. Both were classified as *must-have* despite arising after the planned scope was complete, because a data-leakage defect in a system whose entire purpose is controlled access cannot be deferred.

### 20.2 Defect Discovery Through Review Testing

The defects were found not by the automated test suite but by deliberate **review testing**, in which the tester persona actively attempted to do things the system should forbid, rather than confirming that permitted actions work. This distinction matters and is the iteration's first lesson. The automated tests written during the three increments were, appropriately, *positive* tests: they confirmed that an administrator could see the dashboard, that a lead investigator could rank items by risk, that the assistant answered questions. None of them asked the adversarial question — *can a user see data they should not?* — because each test constructed only the data it needed and then checked that the feature worked on it. The leakage was invisible to that style of testing because it only manifests when data from *another* case exists and a user without access to it views an aggregate.

The discovery came from switching hats consciously. Logged in as a lead investigator who was a member of one case, the tester opened the dashboard and observed that its charts and its "top highest-risk evidence" table counted and displayed items from cases the investigator was not a member of. The same probing of the risk dashboard and the anomaly view showed the same global scope. Turning to the AI assistant, the tester asked it to list all cases and received a complete list of every case in the system, including those the investigator had no access to; and, more subtly, after logging out and logging back in as a different user on the same browser session, the previous user's conversation was still present. Each of these is an authorisation boundary that the rest of the system enforced correctly for direct record access but that the *aggregate* and *conversational* surfaces had quietly bypassed. That the case list and the individual evidence pages already scoped correctly, while the dashboards and the assistant did not, is what made the defect a genuine oversight rather than a design gap: the pattern for correct scoping existed in the codebase; it simply had not been applied to every surface that needed it.

### 20.3 Change Request CR-01 / US-28 — Role-Scoped Dashboard and Aggregated Views

**The defect.** The dashboard, the risk dashboard, and the anomaly view queried every case, evidence item, and audit record in the system regardless of the viewing investigator's role or case memberships. A non-administrator therefore saw, in aggregate form, counts and rankings and charts computed over data they had no right to see individually — a leak that is arguably worse than direct record access because it is silent and pervasive, appearing on the first screen every user sees after logging in.

**Root cause.** The dashboard and risk routes had been written, in Increment 3, to query the entities globally — the count of all cases, all evidence items scored and ranked, all audit records charted — because at the time they were written the case-scoping concern had been associated with the case-list and record-detail routes rather than recognised as a property every aggregate must share. There was no single place that expressed "the set of cases this user may see"; the case list computed it inline, and nothing else reused it.

**The solution.** The fix introduced exactly that missing abstraction: a single helper, `accessible_case_ids`, that returns the set of case identifiers a given investigator may see — a sentinel meaning "all cases" for an administrator, the list of the investigator's memberships for anyone else, and an empty list for a user who belongs to no case. Every aggregate query in the system was then routed through this helper, so that the dashboard's counts and charts, the risk dashboard's ranking, the anomaly pipeline's item set, and the per-item risk and audit-formula endpoints all compute over only the cases the viewer may access, with the per-item endpoints returning an authorisation error for a cross-case request. Centralising the rule in one helper is the essential engineering move: it means the scoping is defined once and applied uniformly, and that a future aggregate view cannot accidentally omit it without the omission being conspicuous.

**Beyond the fix: role-tailored dashboards.** The change request went further than plugging the leak, because scoping the dashboard raised a design question the original global dashboard had never had to answer: what should a Read-Only user with no shared cases *see*, if not everything? A naive scoping would have left such a user with a blank page. The solution turned the constraint into a feature by making the dashboard **role-tailored**. Each role now sees panels matched to its work: an administrator sees system-wide totals and a security-alerts panel of locked accounts and recent failed logins; a lead investigator sees a pending-actions panel of flagged items and items awaiting a lifecycle step in their cases; an analyst sees a workbench of the items they currently hold, with shortcuts to verify, transfer, and change state; and a Read-Only auditor sees a review queue of recently updated items in their shared cases. Every role additionally has a "your recent activity" panel drawn from their own audit records, which guarantees that no dashboard is ever empty, and a user who genuinely belongs to no case sees an explanatory message directing them to ask an administrator or lead for access rather than a blank or, worse, a global view. This turns the least-privilege constraint from a limitation into a more usable, role-appropriate interface — an example of a security requirement improving rather than degrading the user experience.

### 20.4 Change Request CR-02 / US-29 — Access-Scoped AI Assistant and Chat Isolation

**The defects.** The AI assistant exhibited a cluster of related problems. Its context-retrieval stage queried the most recent cases, evidence, and transfers *globally*, so that a lead investigator could ask it to list all cases and receive every case in the system. It could not answer identity questions such as "who am I" or "what are my cases", because it had no notion of the current user. Its conversation history persisted in the session across logout, so that on a shared browser one investigator's chat became visible to the next person to log in. And its responses took one to two minutes, because the largest available model was being called with no request timeout, so a slow call hung the interface.

**Root causes.** The retrieval leak had the same root cause as the dashboard leak — the assistant's queries had been written globally in Increment 3 without routing through the case-access rule, which at that point did not yet exist as a reusable helper. The identity gap was simply an unimplemented capability: the pipeline was never given the current user, so it could not reason about "me". The history leak was a session-management oversight: the chat history was stored under a session key that logout did not clear and that login did not reset, so it survived a change of user. The latency was a configuration choice — the largest model and no timeout — that had been acceptable in isolated testing but not in use.

**The solutions.** Each problem was addressed at its root. Retrieval was routed through the same `accessible_case_ids` helper introduced by CR-01, so the assistant now retrieves only data from the user's accessible cases and a request for a case outside that set returns an access-denied line rather than data; because the retrieval is scoped, the hallucination check inherits a second duty, flagging any entity the model names that the user was not permitted to see. Identity was handled by adding an *identity* intent, checked with priority so that a self-referential question is recognised even though words like "cases" also belong to other intents, and by injecting the authenticated user's identity into the prompt so that "who am I" and "my cases" are answered from the logged-in user and their accessible cases. History isolation was achieved by tagging the stored conversation with the owning user's identifier and clearing it on both login and logout, so that a session left behind by one user can never surface another's conversation. The latency was reduced by defaulting to a smaller, faster model and adding a request timeout on the model call together with an abort in the browser, so that a slow response now fails cleanly with a message rather than hanging.

### 20.5 Regression Testing

The iteration's second lesson is embodied in its testing. The defects had escaped precisely because the existing tests were positive-only, so the fixes were accompanied by a dedicated test module of fourteen tests written in the *adversarial* style that would have caught the defects in the first place. These tests construct a scenario with two leads, an analyst, and a read-only auditor, and two separate cases with membership deliberately arranged so that one lead and the analyst belong to the first case and the other lead to the second, while the auditor belongs to neither. They then assert the negative properties directly: that the `accessible_case_ids` helper returns the right set for each role, that a lead's dashboard and risk dashboard contain no trace of the case they do not belong to, that a read-only user with no memberships sees the explanatory empty state rather than any data, that a cross-case per-item risk request is refused, that the assistant will not list or describe an inaccessible case and answers identity questions from the correct user, and that one user's chat history does not survive into another user's session. Alongside this new module, the full existing suite was re-run to confirm that scoping every aggregate had not broken any previously passing behaviour. The combined result — the new adversarial tests passing and the entire prior suite still green — is what allowed the iteration to be considered done under the same definition of done applied to the increments.

### 20.6 Iteration Review and Reflection

The iteration delivered both change requests, closed the two defects, and left the system with a uniform case-scoping rule applied to every surface, a role-tailored dashboard, an access-aware assistant, and a regression suite that tests the authorisation boundaries adversarially. Its reflection produced two durable lessons for the project. The first is about testing: positive functional tests, however thorough, do not exercise authorisation boundaries, and a system with an access-control requirement needs *negative* tests that actively attempt forbidden actions — a practice the project adopted from this point on. The second is about abstraction and its timing: the leakage existed because the case-scoping rule had been expressed inline in one place rather than as a reusable helper, so that later surfaces could not reuse it and silently omitted it; introducing `accessible_case_ids` as the single source of that rule not only fixed the present defects but made the class of defect much harder to reintroduce, because the correct behaviour is now the easy, obvious thing to call. Both lessons are of the kind that only surface once a working system exists to be probed, which is itself the argument for the incremental model: it produces working software early enough that its real weaknesses can be found and fixed while the project is still under way rather than after it has shipped.

## 21. Maintenance Iteration M2 — Correctness and Security Hardening

### 21.1 Origin: a Structured Code-Review Pass

Where M1 was triggered by exploratory review testing, M2 was triggered by a deliberate, structured **code review** of the whole system carried out after the access-scoping fixes had settled. The review read the accepted proposal against the implementation, walked every logic module, model, and route, and produced a findings document that catalogued each defect together with its cause and a recommended remedy, without changing any code. Reviewing a working system against its own specification is a distinct engineering activity from testing it: testing asks whether the system does what a test expects, whereas review asks whether the code *means* what it appears to mean and whether it honours the intent of the requirements. Several of the defects M2 fixed are of a kind that testing is structurally poor at catching — a feature that silently does nothing, a computation that reads a field that does not exist, a permission check applied to the wrong identifier — because the positive tests around them pass while the underlying behaviour is wrong. This is the complement of M1's lesson: M1 showed that authorisation defects need adversarial testing, and M2 shows that certain correctness defects need reading rather than running.

The iteration grouped its findings into three themes — correctness defects, security hardening, and robustness improvements — summarised in Table 21.1 and detailed in the sections that follow. As with M1, every fix was accompanied by tests and validated against the full regression suite before being considered done.

**Table 21.1 — M2 findings overview**

| # | Finding | Theme | Severity |
|---|---|---|---|
| D1 | Evidence deletion set a non-existent attribute and persisted nothing | Correctness | Critical |
| D2 | The highest-weighted risk factor read a column that did not exist | Correctness | Critical |
| D3 | Cross-case access to evidence via a mismatched URL identifier | Correctness / Security | Critical |
| D4 | Custody signing failure could abort a transfer or leave an unverifiable record | Correctness | High |
| D5 | Two risk factors counted the wrong audit events, rewarding scrutiny with risk | Correctness | High |
| D6 | Mixed timezone-aware and naive timestamps risked comparison errors | Correctness | Medium |
| H1 | Untrusted model output rendered as HTML in the chat (stored XSS) | Security | High |
| H2 | No Content-Security-Policy or complementary hardening headers | Security | Medium |
| H3 | No warning when the app ran with insecure default secrets | Security | Low |
| R1 | Unbounded case and evidence listings | Robustness | Low |
| R2 | Intent classifier mis-ranked self-referential questions | Robustness | Low |

### 21.2 Correctness Defects

**D1 — Evidence deletion did nothing.** The delete-evidence route set an `is_deleted` attribute on the evidence object and wrote an audit record stating that the item had been deleted, but the Evidence Item entity had no such column. SQLAlchemy silently accepted the assignment as a transient Python attribute that the commit did not persist, so the item remained fully visible everywhere while the audit trail now contained a false statement that it had been removed. For a chain-of-custody system this is the worst category of defect, because it makes the audit trail — the system's most trusted artefact — lie. The fix added a real `is_deleted` boolean column to the entity and to the startup schema migration, changed the route to set it and to describe the action honestly as a soft delete, and filtered deleted items out of every listing path — the case view, the dashboards, the risk and anomaly views, and the export. Soft deletion is the correct pattern here rather than a hard delete, because destroying an item's row would orphan its custody and audit history, and in a forensic system the fact that an item was deleted, and by whom, is itself part of the record that must be preserved.

**D2 — The highest-weighted risk factor was dead.** The risk scorer, the anomaly feature builder, and the dashboard all read an `integrity_status` attribute from the evidence item, but no such column existed and nothing ever wrote one. The consequence was severe and silent: the integrity factor, weighted highest of the eight at 0.25, returned a constant for every item, so a quarter of the flagship risk algorithm's output was noise; the interaction rules that depended on integrity could never fire; and the dashboard's integrity panel showed every item as unchecked even after verifications had run. The fix added a real `integrity_status` column, with values verified, not-checked, and failed, and wrote it back from the graph verifier: a verification that returns an Intact verdict sets the status to verified, a Broken verdict sets it to failed, and a partial result leaves it not-checked. This single change brought a quarter of the risk algorithm to life and closed the loop between the Increment 2 integrity verifier and the Increment 3 risk scorer, so that a failed verification now genuinely raises an item's risk.

**D3 — Cross-case evidence access.** The routes that view and edit an evidence item checked the requesting user's access against the case identifier taken from the URL, but loaded the evidence item purely by its own identifier without confirming that the item actually belonged to that case. Any user who was a member of even one case could therefore view, and even edit the metadata of, any evidence item in the system by requesting it under the path of a case they did belong to — an insecure-direct-object-reference defect that defeated the case-access layer for the system's central entity. The custody and risk routes had avoided this by deriving the case from the evidence record itself; the fix brought the evidence routes into line, verifying that the loaded item's case matches the access that was checked, so that an item can only be reached through the case it truly belongs to.

**D4 — Custody signing failure handling.** The signing paths had two related weaknesses: a transfer whose signing produced no signature would attempt to write a null into the non-nullable signature column and raise a database error that aborted the transfer, and an upload in the same situation risked leaving the chain permanently unverifiable. The fix standardised the fail-visible behaviour described in Part III: both upload and transfer events are signed in normal operation, and if signing is ever unavailable the system records an explicit unsigned sentinel and warns the user rather than crashing, with the graph verifier reporting such a link as unverified. This removes the crash while keeping an unsigned event conspicuous rather than silent.

**D5 — Risk factors counting the wrong events.** Two of the eight risk factors were counting the wrong audit records. The duplicate-entry factor counted integrity-check audit events and the role-mismatch factor counted any warning-level audit event, with the effect that every time anyone ran a verification the item's "duplicate" and "role-mismatch" risk rose — the system was rewarding scrutiny with higher risk, the opposite of the intended meaning, while the genuine duplicate and role-mismatch detectors built in Increment 1 were computed only on demand and never reached the scorer. The fix pointed the two factors at the real Category A formula checks, so that the risk scorer now counts genuine duplicate custody entries and genuine role-permission mismatches computed live by the audit formulas. Beyond fixing the defect, this produced the deliberate cross-category integration described in Part IV, in which the simple Category A formulas feed the complex Category C algorithm.

**D6 — Timestamp consistency.** A custody record's default timestamp was produced as a timezone-aware UTC value while the rest of the system stored naive local timestamps through the shared time helper, so that comparisons between the two — in the graph verifier's monotonicity check and in the risk scorer's gap calculations — risked raising an error about mixing aware and naive datetimes, or introducing a silent offset. The fix routed the custody timestamp default through the same local-time helper as every other model, so that the whole system now uses one consistent, naive local representation and the comparison hazard is removed.

### 21.3 Security Hardening

**H1 — Stored cross-site scripting in the chat.** The AI chat interface rendered the model's reply, and any replayed history, as HTML in order to display simple formatting. Because the model's output is untrusted — a prompt-injection payload placed in a case title or description could steer it — rendering that output as HTML created a stored cross-site-scripting vector. The fix escapes the model's output so that it is displayed as text rather than interpreted as markup, closing the vector while preserving the intended lightweight formatting through a safe transformation applied after escaping.

**H2 — Content-Security-Policy and hardening headers.** The application sent a transport-security header but no content-security policy. The fix added a Content-Security-Policy that restricts where scripts, styles, fonts, and images may load from — reflecting exactly what the application actually uses — together with the complementary `X-Content-Type-Options`, `X-Frame-Options`, and `Referrer-Policy` headers, providing defence-in-depth against script injection and clickjacking. The policy is documented as still permitting inline scripts and styles because the templates embed them, with the tightening of that allowance recorded as a known next step rather than claimed as complete.

**H3 — Insecure default-secret warnings.** The application would run with built-in development defaults for its session secret and its key-encryption secret, which is convenient for development but dangerous if it reached a real deployment unnoticed. The fix emits a clear startup warning whenever either secret is using its insecure default, so that the condition is impossible to overlook — the same warning that appears, correctly, throughout the test output.

### 21.4 Robustness Improvements

Two smaller improvements rounded out the iteration. The case and evidence listings, previously unbounded, were **paginated** so that the views stay responsive as the data grows; the sequential numbering scheme for cases and evidence was left as it was, because the soft-delete change means deleted rows still count and the scheme therefore remains collision-free in the single-user operation the system targets, with its behaviour under concurrency recorded honestly as a known limitation rather than papered over. The **intent classifier** was given a priority rule so that a clearly self-referential question such as "what are the cases assigned to me?" is recognised as an identity query even though it also contains words that belong to the general case-query intent, and this behaviour was pinned down with a dedicated test module so that the classifier's ranking cannot silently regress.

### 21.5 Schema Migration and Regression Testing

Two of the correctness fixes — the soft-delete flag and the integrity status — added columns to the Evidence Item entity, and a database created under the earlier schema would lack them. Rather than requiring the database to be rebuilt, the two columns were added to the lightweight startup migration routine introduced in Increment 1, which inspects the existing columns and adds any that are missing, so that an existing database is brought up to date automatically. Every fix in the iteration was accompanied by tests — the intent classifier gained its own module, and the correctness fixes were exercised through the existing increment and integration suites — and the complete suite was re-run to confirmation. The final result is a suite of one hundred and seventy-two tests, all passing, across the increment, integration, scoping, and intent-classifier modules, which is the state in which the system stands at the time of writing.

### 21.6 Iteration Review and Reflection

M2 delivered eleven fixes across correctness, security, and robustness, and left the system materially more trustworthy than the version that had passed all its tests at the end of Increment 3. Its central reflection complements M1's. M1 taught that authorisation defects require adversarial testing; M2 taught that a distinct class of correctness defect — a feature that silently does nothing, a computation over a field that does not exist, a check against the wrong identifier — is best caught by reading the code against its intent, because the surrounding tests pass while the behaviour is quietly wrong. That two of M2's critical findings, the dead delete and the dead integrity factor, had survived a full increment and a green test suite is not an indictment of the testing but an illustration of its limits: a test confirms that the code does what the test expects, and neither of these defects was in the path of any expectation. The practical conclusion the project drew is that testing and review are complementary rather than substitutable — the suite guards against regression in behaviour that is exercised, while periodic review guards against behaviour that is specified but silently absent — and that a system whose correctness genuinely matters warrants both. Taken together, the two maintenance iterations demonstrate the incremental model's central claim in miniature: because working software existed to be tested adversarially and read critically, the project's real weaknesses were found and repaired as part of the process rather than discovered after delivery.
