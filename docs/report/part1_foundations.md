## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Project Background

Modern criminal and corporate investigations increasingly turn on *digital* evidence: disk images, memory dumps, log files, photographs, intercepted messages, database extracts, and the metadata that surrounds them. Unlike a physical exhibit sealed in an evidence bag, a digital artefact can be copied perfectly, altered without visible trace, and moved between investigators in seconds. This fluidity is precisely what makes digital evidence powerful and, at the same time, fragile as a matter of legal procedure. Courts admit an exhibit only when the party presenting it can demonstrate an unbroken *chain of custody*: a complete, tamper-evident record of who collected the item, who has held it at every moment since, what was done to it, and proof that its content has not changed between collection and presentation. A single unexplained gap — an hour during which no one is recorded as the custodian, a transfer with no signature, a file whose hash no longer matches the value recorded at seizure — is often sufficient for the evidence to be ruled inadmissible, regardless of how incriminating its content might be.

In practice, the chain of custody has historically been maintained on paper forms and spreadsheets. That approach scales poorly and fails silently: a spreadsheet cannot cryptographically prove that a file is unchanged, cannot prevent an unauthorised analyst from editing a record, and cannot detect that a custody entry has been deleted after the fact. The people who most need these guarantees — forensic investigators, evidence custodians, and the legal teams that rely on their work — are not usually in a position to build bespoke software, and general-purpose document systems do not encode the domain rules (who may hold which category of evidence, which lifecycle transitions are legally meaningful, how integrity is proven) that a forensic workflow requires.

DEICMS — the Digital Evidence Integrity & Chain-of-Custody Management System — is a response to that gap. It is a web application in which every action that touches an evidence item is authenticated, authorised against a role and a case membership, cryptographically signed where a hand-off of responsibility occurs, and written to an append-only audit trail. Around this secure core, the system layers analytical capabilities: it verifies the integrity of an entire custody history as a graph rather than a single latest record, scores each item for risk across multiple interacting signals, reconstructs plausible custody chains where records are incomplete, and offers investigators a natural-language assistant that answers questions strictly from the data they are permitted to see. The project was undertaken as the coursework for the Software Engineering module, and it was deliberately chosen because the problem domain maps cleanly and honestly onto the three feature categories the coursework requires, giving a balanced vehicle for demonstrating data modelling, third-party integration, and genuinely complex algorithmic work.

### 1.2 Problem Statement

The concrete problem DEICMS addresses can be stated as a set of guarantees that a credible digital-evidence system must provide and that ad-hoc tools do not.

First, **integrity must be provable, not merely asserted.** It is not enough to store a file and claim it is unchanged; the system must be able to demonstrate, for any point in an item's history, that the content at that moment matched the content recorded at seizure, and that each custody record was created by the investigator it names. This demands cryptographic hashing at intake and digital signatures on custody events, and — crucially — a verification procedure that examines the *whole* chain rather than trusting that if the latest record looks correct the earlier ones must be too.

Second, **access must follow the principle of least privilege at the granularity of a case.** A forensic organisation runs many investigations simultaneously, and an analyst assigned to one case has no legitimate need to see, let alone modify, the evidence of another. A coarse "logged-in users can see everything" model is unacceptable; access must be scoped to the specific cases an investigator is a member of, and every aggregate view — dashboards, risk rankings, even an AI assistant's answers — must respect that boundary rather than leaking data across it.

Third, **the record must be tamper-evident and complete.** Audit records must be append-only: it must be impossible, through the application, to edit or delete the history of what happened. Deleting an evidence item must not destroy its custody and audit history, because that history may itself be evidence of misconduct.

Fourth, **procedural rules must be enforced by the system, not left to discipline.** Evidence moves through a lifecycle — collected, submitted, analysed, transferred, archived — and only certain transitions are legally meaningful, only certain roles may trigger them, and some require preconditions (for example, an item should not be archived until its integrity has been verified). Encoding these as a formal state machine turns a matter of training and vigilance into a matter of enforced logic.

Fifth, and more analytically, **investigators need help prioritising.** A large investigation may involve hundreds of evidence items; a human cannot manually review every audit record to decide which items are most at risk of having been mishandled or tampered with. The system should combine multiple independent signals into a defensible risk score and surface the highest-risk items first, while being honest about the limits of that automation.

These five guarantees — provable integrity, case-scoped least-privilege access, a tamper-evident complete record, enforced procedural rules, and analytical prioritisation — constitute the problem DEICMS was built to solve, and they recur throughout this report as the criteria against which each increment's work is measured.

### 1.3 Objectives

The project set the following objectives, which also served as the yardstick for the definition of done at the level of the system as a whole:

The **primary functional objective** was to deliver a working web application that manages the full lifecycle of digital evidence within cases, enforces role-based and case-based access control, and maintains a cryptographically verifiable chain of custody with an append-only audit trail. The **algorithmic objective** was to design and implement, without relying on a library to do the interesting work, at least three non-trivial algorithms — settled as the graph integrity verifier, the multi-factor risk scorer, and the custody-chain reconstructor — together with a formal lifecycle state machine and an AI orchestration workflow, each described well enough that its complexity is demonstrable rather than merely claimed. The **integration objective** was to consume established third-party services (cryptography, metadata extraction, a pre-trained language model, an anomaly-detection model, and PDF generation) correctly and to be honest about the boundary between what those services provide and what the surrounding application logic contributes. The **process objective** was to conduct the work as a genuine agile incremental project, producing per-increment planning and reflection artefacts, and to document the software development process in enough depth to satisfy the reporting requirement. Finally, the **quality objective** was to validate the system with an automated test suite of meaningful coverage and to harden it against the common web-application vulnerabilities that a forensic system, of all things, cannot afford to exhibit.

### 1.4 Scope and Limitations

DEICMS is scoped as an academic project intended to demonstrate software-engineering practice, and its boundaries were drawn deliberately. **In scope** are: the six-entity relational model and its CRUD operations; authentication with optional two-factor authentication, session management, and account lockout; role-based and case-based authorisation; file upload with hashing, metadata extraction, content-type validation, and encryption at rest; the full custody-transfer workflow with digital signatures; the evidence lifecycle state machine; the three complex algorithms and the anomaly-detection pipeline; the AI investigation assistant; role-scoped dashboards and reporting including a signed export package and a per-item PDF custody report; and the append-only audit trail with formula-based checks.

**Out of scope**, and acknowledged as such, are a number of concerns that a production deployment would require but that do not add to the software-engineering demonstration. The system targets SQLite for development and is written to be PostgreSQL-ready through SQLAlchemy, but it is not deployed to a production database or clustered. It serves over HTTPS using a self-signed certificate suitable for local demonstration rather than a certificate from a public authority. It does not implement email or push notifications, full-text search over evidence *content* (as opposed to metadata), a multi-language interface, a mobile client, or fine-tuning of the language model. These exclusions are recorded explicitly in the product backlog's "won't-have" list so that the boundary of the work is transparent.

The principal **limitations** worth stating up front are two. The language model is consumed as a third-party service and, like all such models, can produce plausible but incorrect text; DEICMS mitigates this with a hallucination-detection stage that cross-checks the model's output against retrieved data, but it does not eliminate the underlying risk, and the assistant is positioned as a convenience rather than an authority. The anomaly-detection model is an unsupervised Isolation Forest trained on a small dataset; its output is therefore presented as "suggested for review" alongside the deterministic risk score rather than as a confirmed judgement. Both limitations are treated as design constraints rather than defects, and the way the system communicates them to the user is itself part of the design.

## 2. Software Development Process

### 2.1 Choice of Process Model

The first substantive engineering decision on the project was the choice of software development process, because that choice shapes how every subsequent activity is planned, sequenced, and documented. The realistic candidates were a plan-driven (waterfall) model, in which requirements, design, implementation, and testing proceed as sequential phases each completed before the next begins, and an agile incremental model, in which the system is grown in a series of short cycles, each of which passes through all of those activities and delivers a working, testable increment of functionality.

The project adopted the **Agile Incremental Model**, organised into three increments, for reasons that were specific to the nature of the work rather than a general preference for agility. The decisive factor was **technical risk concentrated in the complex algorithms.** The graph integrity verifier, the risk scorer, and the chain reconstructor were the parts of the system whose feasibility and shape were least certain at the outset; a waterfall plan would have committed their full design on paper before any of them had been built, and the near-certainty of discovering, during implementation, that the design needed to change would have invalidated large amounts of up-front documentation. An incremental approach let the data-model and CRUD foundation be built and stabilised first (Increment 1), so that the algorithms in Increment 2 could be designed against a concrete schema and validated with real records, and so that the analytical and AI features in Increment 3 could build on verified integrity primitives. Each increment delivered something that actually ran and could be demonstrated, which meant that risk was retired continuously rather than accumulating until a single late integration.

A second factor was **the value of feedback between increments.** The retrospective at the end of each increment produced concrete process adjustments that measurably improved the next one — a pattern documented in detail in the per-increment chapters and summarised in Part VII. A plan-driven model has no natural place for that mid-project learning. A third factor was **reporting fit**: the coursework requires the report to be organised according to the chosen process, with a section per increment for an agile model, and the incremental structure produces exactly the planning-and-reflection artefacts (user stories, sprint backlogs, burndown charts, retrospectives) that make an agile report substantial and honest.

The model was not adopted uncritically. The project was carried out by a single developer, so the collaborative ceremonies of a team-based agile method (daily stand-ups across people, collective estimation) were adapted into individual equivalents: estimation was done by planning-poker-style reasoning against a Fibonacci scale rather than by group consensus, and the "stand-up" became a short daily written note of yesterday/today/blockers. This adaptation is stated openly because pretending to team ceremonies that did not occur would undermine the honesty the report aims for.

### 2.2 The Incremental Plan

The work was divided into three increments, each a fourteen-working-day cycle, plus two subsequent maintenance iterations. The increments were sequenced so that each depended only on what preceded it.

**Increment 1 — Core Case & Evidence Management** established the foundation: all five original database entities plus the case-access table, full CRUD operations, authentication and role-based access control, and file upload with SHA-256 hashing and metadata extraction, together with the append-only audit trail and the simple formula-based audit checks. Its deliverable was a system in which an investigator could log in, manage cases, register hashed evidence, and have every action recorded. The UML artefacts produced were the use-case diagram, the class diagram, and the entity-relationship diagram.

**Increment 2 — Integrity Algorithms & State Machine** added the first tier of genuinely complex logic on top of that foundation: Ed25519 digital signing of custody events, the graph-based chain-of-custody integrity verification algorithm, the evidence lifecycle state machine with role-gated and precondition-constrained transitions, and the heuristic custody-chain reconstruction algorithm, each with a supporting user interface. Its UML artefacts were the sequence diagram of the integrity verification, the activity diagram of the reconstruction, and the state diagram of the lifecycle.

**Increment 3 — Risk Scoring, AI Assistant & Hardening** delivered the analytical and assistive layer: the multi-factor risk-scoring algorithm, the AI investigation assistant's orchestration workflow with hallucination detection, the Isolation Forest anomaly-detection pipeline, the risk and anomaly dashboards, the security hardening suite, and the tamper-evident export and PDF reporting. Its UML artefacts were the activity diagram of the risk scoring, the sequence diagram of the AI orchestration, and the component and deployment diagrams of the whole system.

Two **maintenance iterations** followed. The first, M1, resolved two defects discovered during review testing — aggregated views and the AI assistant leaking data across case boundaries — through a change-request process. The second, M2, resolved a set of correctness and security defects — among them a soft delete that silently did nothing and the highest-weighted risk factor reading a non-existent column — found during a final review pass. Both are planned and reported as first-class iterations because change and defect repair after delivery are a normal part of a software product's life, and the incremental model handles them without disruption.

The three increments carried planned budgets of forty, thirty-eight, and forty-two story points respectively, for a total of one hundred and twenty points of committed scope, with the first maintenance iteration adding ten; the second maintenance iteration was defect-repair driven and is described by its findings rather than by a story-point budget. These figures, their per-story breakdown, and the actual versus ideal progress are given in the sprint backlogs and burndown charts presented in the increment chapters.

### 2.3 Roles, Ceremonies and Definition of Done

Because the project had a single developer, the several roles of a conventional agile team — product owner, developer, tester — were held by one person and consciously switched between. Acting as product owner meant maintaining the product backlog, writing user stories with acceptance criteria, and prioritising with MoSCoW; acting as developer meant designing and implementing; acting as tester meant writing the automated test suite and performing the review testing that, in the maintenance iteration, surfaced real defects. Keeping these hats distinct mattered: the access-scoping defects fixed in M1 were found precisely because the "tester" deliberately tried to access another case's data rather than assuming the "developer" had prevented it.

The **ceremonies** were adapted to the single-developer context. Each increment opened with a planning session that selected stories from the product backlog into the sprint backlog and broke them into tasks with hour estimates. A brief daily written note tracked progress and blockers. Each increment closed with a review — a demonstration that the increment's stories met their acceptance criteria — and a retrospective that identified what had gone well, what had not, and concrete actions for the next increment.

The **definition of done** was applied uniformly. A story was considered done only when its code was implemented and integrated into the running application, its acceptance criteria were demonstrably met, it was covered by automated tests that passed, any action that touched evidence was recorded in the audit trail, and no story left the increment in a state that broke another. This last clause is why, for example, the signature column on custody records was made non-nullable in the same change that introduced signing, rather than later: a definition of done that permits an increment to leave the schema in an inconsistent state is not a definition of done.

### 2.4 Tooling and Engineering Practices

The project used a small, deliberately conventional toolchain. Version control was **Git**, with the history serving as an auditable record of how the system evolved; commits were made at logical boundaries with descriptive messages, and the diagrams and documentation were versioned alongside the code so that no artefact could drift out of step with the implementation without the divergence being visible. Automated testing used **pytest** with **pytest-cov** for coverage measurement, and the suite was organised to mirror the increments — one test module per increment plus dedicated modules for integration, access-scoping, and the intent classifier — so that a failure could be localised to the increment whose behaviour it concerned.

Two engineering practices are worth highlighting because they recur in the increment narratives. The first is **test-informed development of the algorithms**: from Increment 3 onward, the acceptance criteria of a complex algorithm were expressed as unit tests before the algorithm was implemented, a practice that caught real edge cases — degenerate normalisation bounds, empty custody chains — before they could reach a route. The second is **designing third-party dependencies behind a seam**: the language model, for instance, is invoked through a single method that falls back to a context-aware mock when no API key is configured, which made the entire six-stage assistant pipeline testable without external credits and insulated the rest of the system from the specifics of the provider. Both practices are examples of the general principle that the parts of the system most likely to change or to fail were the parts given the most isolation and the most tests.

## 3. Requirements Engineering

### 3.1 Stakeholders and Personas

The requirements were elicited by reasoning about the people who would use a digital-evidence system and the distinct responsibilities they hold, and they were captured as four personas that correspond to the system's four roles. The personas are used throughout the user stories so that each requirement is anchored to a concrete need rather than an abstract actor.

**Amelia, the Administrator,** is responsible for the system itself rather than for any single investigation. She creates investigator accounts, assigns and changes roles, unlocks accounts, tunes the risk-scoring configuration, and has oversight of every case. Her defining need is control and visibility across the whole system, and she is the only role permitted to see the global audit trail.

**Luca, the Lead Investigator,** owns cases. He opens investigations, adds members to them, uploads and transfers evidence, drives items through their lifecycle, and produces the court-ready export package at the end. His defining need is to manage his own cases efficiently and to be alerted to items that require his attention, while never being burdened with — or given access to — cases that are not his.

**Nadia, the Analyst,** works on the evidence within the cases she is assigned to. She examines items, runs integrity checks, queries the AI assistant, and holds custody of items during analysis. Her defining need is a focused workbench of the items she is currently responsible for, with the tools to verify and act on them, and nothing beyond her remit.

**Riya, the Read-Only auditor,** reviews without modifying. She inspects custody chains and audit trails in the cases shared with her to confirm that procedure has been followed. Her defining need is transparency: she must be able to see the history of the items she is permitted to review, but she must never be able to change anything, and she must not see cases she has not been given access to.

These four personas embody the least-privilege principle that runs through the whole system: each sees and can do exactly what their responsibility requires, and the difference between them is enforced by the role and case-access model rather than by convention.

### 3.2 Functional Requirements

The functional requirements were derived from the personas and the five guarantees of the problem statement, and they are recorded as user stories in the product backlog. In summary form, the system shall provide the following capabilities. It shall authenticate investigators, optionally with a second factor, and manage their sessions with an idle timeout and lockout after repeated failures. It shall let administrators create accounts and assign one of four roles, and it shall enforce those roles on every route. It shall let authorised users create, search, update, and archive cases, and it shall scope every user's view of cases to those they are members of. It shall register uploaded files as evidence items, fingerprinting each with SHA-256 at intake, extracting metadata, validating declared content types against actual bytes, and encrypting stored files at rest. It shall record every custody transfer and access, signing custody events with the outgoing investigator's private key, and it shall present the custody log per item. It shall maintain an append-only audit trail of every significant event and expose it per item, per investigator, and — for administrators — globally, and it shall run formula-based checks for custody time-gaps, duplicate entries, and role-permission mismatches.

Beyond these data-oriented capabilities, the system shall verify the integrity of an item's entire custody history and report a verdict and completeness score; it shall enforce the evidence lifecycle as a formal state machine with role-gated, precondition-constrained transitions; it shall reconstruct plausible custody chains where records are incomplete and label each link by confidence; it shall score every item for risk across multiple interacting signals and rank items accordingly; it shall flag statistical outliers through an anomaly-detection model; it shall answer investigators' natural-language questions strictly from data they are permitted to see, with a check against fabricated references; and it shall produce a tamper-evident signed export of a case and a printable chain-of-custody report for an item. Each of these is elaborated, with its acceptance criteria, in the increment chapter that delivered it.

### 3.3 Non-Functional Requirements

The non-functional requirements are, for a forensic system, as important as the functional ones, and several of them are properly understood as security requirements. **Confidentiality and least privilege** require that no user ever see case data outside their membership, a requirement that applies not only to direct record access but to every aggregate — dashboards, risk rankings, exports, and the AI assistant's answers. **Integrity** requires that evidence content be verifiable against its intake hash and that custody records be non-repudiable through digital signatures. **Auditability** requires that the record of what happened be complete and append-only, resistant to modification through the application. **Availability and robustness** require that the system degrade gracefully — for example, that the AI assistant fall back to a usable response when its external service is unavailable, and that a slow model call not hang the interface indefinitely. **Usability** requires that each role be presented with an interface matched to its work, so that the system is not merely secure but actually usable by the people it is for. **Maintainability** requires that the code be organised so that a change is localised: the application-factory-and-blueprint structure, the separation of business logic from routes, and the single source of truth for cross-cutting concerns such as local time all serve this requirement. Finally, **portability** requires that the persistence layer not be tied to a single database engine, which is achieved by using an object-relational mapper and writing no raw engine-specific SQL in the application logic.

### 3.4 Feature Categorisation (A / B / C) and Balance Rationale

The coursework requires that the system's functionality be categorised into three kinds — data/repository, third-party services, and complex application logic — and that the proposal be balanced across them. This categorisation was performed honestly, with each feature assigned to exactly one category on the basis of where its essential difficulty lies, and it is worth setting out here because it explains the shape of the whole system.

**Category A — data / repository / DB-oriented functionality.** These features store, retrieve, update, and delete structured records, or compute straightforward formula-based values directly over those records. The complexity lives in the data model and its relationships, not in any application-level algorithm. In DEICMS this category comprises the CRUD operations over the six entities (Investigator, Case, Evidence Item, Custody Log, Audit Record, and Case Access), case search and archiving, the append-only audit trail, and the three formula checks — custody time-gap detection, duplicate-entry detection, and role-permission-mismatch detection — each of which is a direct arithmetic or set-membership computation over database fields.

**Category B — third-party services and libraries.** These features consume an external library or pre-trained model as-is: the application invokes an interface but does not implement the underlying algorithm. In DEICMS this category comprises SHA-256 hashing (Python's `hashlib`), Ed25519 digital signatures and AES-256-GCM encryption (the `cryptography` library), EXIF and MIME metadata extraction (Pillow and python-magic), the object-relational mapper (SQLAlchemy), the large language model that provides the assistant's natural-language capability (consumed through the NVIDIA NIM API), the Isolation Forest anomaly-detection model (scikit-learn), PDF generation (ReportLab), and TOTP two-factor authentication (PyOTP). In each case the report is careful to distinguish the third-party contribution from the application logic that surrounds it.

**Category C — complex application logic implemented from scratch.** These features are algorithmically non-trivial and were designed and implemented by the developer: they involve a custom data structure, a multi-step algorithm with branching logic, or a heuristic search that cannot be expressed as a simple query, formula, or library call. In DEICMS this category comprises five items: the chain-of-custody graph integrity verification algorithm, the multi-factor evidence risk-scoring algorithm, the custody-chain reconstruction algorithm, the evidence lifecycle state machine, and the AI investigation assistant's orchestration workflow. Where these features call a Category B library — the integrity verifier calls `hashlib` and the signature primitives, the assistant calls the language model — the library plays the role of a primitive operation inside a larger algorithm that the developer designed, in the same way that arithmetic operators are primitives inside a formula; the graph construction, the traversal, the multi-condition validation, the scoring, the search, and the orchestration are the developer's own work.

The **balance** across these categories was a design goal in its own right, and it is assessed in full in Part VI. In brief, the system is not top-heavy in any one category: the data layer is a rich six-entity model rather than a token database, the third-party integrations are numerous and varied rather than a single API call, and the complex-logic layer contributes five substantial algorithms rather than one. The categorisation also produced a deliberate *integration* between categories that strengthens the design — the Category A formula checks feed the Category C risk scorer, so that the simple duplicate and role-mismatch computations become inputs to the complex scoring algorithm rather than sitting in isolation.

### 3.5 Prioritisation and the Product Backlog

The requirements were prioritised using **MoSCoW** — must-have, should-have, could-have, and won't-have-this-time — and recorded as a product backlog of user stories, each estimated in story points on a Fibonacci scale. The must-haves are the features without which the system fails to meet its core purpose: authentication and access control, case and evidence CRUD, hashing, custody logging, the audit trail, digital signing, the graph integrity verifier, the state machine, the reconstruction algorithm, the risk scorer, and the AI assistant with its hallucination check. The should-haves add substantial value but the system would still be coherent without any one of them: the formula checks, the report user interfaces, the configurable risk weights, the dashboards, the two-factor authentication and hardening, and the signed export. The could-haves are genuinely optional enhancements: the Isolation Forest anomaly detection and the PDF custody report. The won't-haves are the out-of-scope items listed in Section 1.4, recorded explicitly so that the boundary of the release is transparent.

Each story carries acceptance criteria that make "done" objective, a MoSCoW priority, a story-point estimate, a feature-category tag, and the increment in which it was delivered. The full catalogue of twenty-nine stories — the twenty-seven original stories plus the two change-request stories from the first maintenance iteration — is reproduced in the appendix, and the stories are presented with their acceptance criteria in the planning chapter of each increment; the second maintenance iteration's work is catalogued as defect findings rather than as backlog stories. The backlog totalled one hundred and twenty points of original scope, distributed as forty, thirty-eight, and forty-two points across the three increments, with a further ten points in the first maintenance iteration; the distribution was chosen so that no increment was overloaded and so that the riskiest work — the algorithms in Increment 2 — sat in the middle, after the foundation was stable but before the project's schedule pressure peaked.

### 3.6 Estimation Method

Because the sprint backlogs and burndown charts that follow are expressed in story points, it is worth setting out precisely how those numbers were arrived at, so that the figures quoted throughout the report are understood as the product of a method rather than as arbitrary values.

Estimation used **story points on a Fibonacci scale** — 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13 — rather than hours. The distinction matters. An hour estimate claims to predict how long something will take, which is a prediction developers are famously poor at making; a story point claims only that one piece of work is about twice the size of another, which is a judgement people make far more reliably. The Fibonacci sequence is used because its gaps widen as the numbers grow: it is possible to distinguish a 3 from a 5 with some confidence, but nobody can honestly distinguish a 7 from an 8, and the absence of those intermediate values prevents false precision. The widening gaps also encode a real property of estimation — that uncertainty grows with size, so large items deserve coarser buckets.

Each point value was given a consistent meaning, applied uniformly across all twenty-nine stories, as set out in Table 3.1.

**Table 3.1 — Story-point scale and its application**

| Points | Meaning | Representative stories |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Trivial; a single small piece that reuses existing patterns | US-11 admin-only account creation |
| 2 | Simple; essentially a library call plus its wiring | US-05 SHA-256 fingerprint, US-26 signed ZIP export, US-27 PDF report, US-16 and US-17 report interfaces |
| 3 | Small; a few parts, but no new design work | US-01 login, US-06 metadata extraction, US-10 formula checks, US-19 configurable weights, US-21 hallucination detection, US-23 dashboards |
| 5 | Moderate; multi-part and substantial, but well understood | US-02 role-based access control, US-03 case CRUD, US-04 evidence upload, US-08 custody logging, US-09 audit trail, US-12 digital signing, US-22 anomaly detection, US-24 two-factor authentication |
| 8 | Large; genuine design work, typically a new algorithm | US-14 state machine, US-15 chain reconstruction, US-18 risk scoring, US-20 AI orchestration |
| 13 | Very large; significant unknowns at the time of estimation | US-13 graph integrity verification |

Estimates were assigned **relatively**, anchored on a reference story rather than judged in isolation. US-05, the SHA-256 fingerprint — call a hashing function, store the result in a column, display it — served as the natural 2, and every other story was sized by asking whether it was about the same, roughly twice as large, or several times larger. This anchoring is what keeps a scale internally consistent when one person applies it across several months.

The resulting distribution is itself evidence that the method was applied honestly rather than retrofitted. Every story estimated at 8 is one of the five complex Category C algorithms; every story estimated at 2 is a thin wrapper over a third-party library; and the single 13 in the entire project went to the graph integrity verifier, the one item whose difficulty genuinely could not be predicted in advance. That last judgement proved correct in a way the estimate could not have anticipated: US-13 is precisely where Increment 2's three-day mid-sprint plateau occurred, as the burndown chart in Chapter 14 records.

It is important to note that **story points and MoSCoW priority are independent dimensions**, even though they appear side by side in the same backlog tables. A point value answers *how large is this?*; a MoSCoW priority answers *what happens if time runs out?*. The two do not correlate, and deliberately so. US-11, admin-only account creation, is the smallest story in the project at 1 point yet is a Must, because a forensic system that permits self-registration is unacceptable regardless of how little code the restriction requires. Conversely US-22, the Isolation Forest anomaly detection, is a 5-point story but only a Could, because it represents real work whose absence would leave the system coherent. Keeping size and importance separate is what allows scope to be cut sensibly under pressure: one drops the least important work, not the largest.

The increment totals quoted throughout this report are **derived quantities rather than planning targets**: each is the arithmetic sum of the estimates of the stories allocated to that increment. Increment 1 totals forty points across eleven stories (3+5+5+5+2+3+3+5+5+3+1), Increment 2 thirty-eight points across six (5+13+8+8+2+2), and Increment 3 forty-two points across ten (8+3+8+3+5+3+5+3+2+2); maintenance iteration M1 contributes a further ten points (5+5), giving a project total of one hundred and thirty points. That the three increment totals fall within the narrow band of thirty-eight to forty-two is a consequence of **capacity rather than coincidence**. Each increment occupied a fixed cycle of fourteen working days undertaken by a single developer, so the volume of work admitted to an increment was bounded by the throughput observed in the preceding one. Increment 1 established that bound empirically by completing forty points within its cycle; forty points per increment therefore became the project's measured velocity, and the two subsequent increments were loaded against that measurement rather than against an assumed rate. This ordering — estimate the work, measure the capacity, then fit the plan to the measurement — is the relationship between estimation and planning prescribed by agile practice, in which velocity is treated as an observed property of the team rather than a target imposed upon it.

The honest caveat, already noted in Section 2.1 and revisited in the velocity analysis of Chapter 25, is that a single-developer project places the estimator and the implementer in the same person, so estimates can be adjusted with perfect knowledge of the implementer's pace. This is why the report treats the *shape* of each burndown chart — where progress stalled and where it recovered — as more informative than the fact that each increment's total was met.

## 4. System-Wide Architecture

### 4.1 Technology Stack and Justification

The technology stack was chosen to be conventional, well-supported, and appropriate to a security-sensitive application, favouring mature libraries with strong track records over novelty. The backend is written in **Python 3** on the **Flask** web framework, a combination selected for Python's exceptionally strong cryptography and machine-learning ecosystems — the two areas where the project needed reliable third-party services — and for Flask's lightness, which keeps the mapping between the application's structure and the framework's mechanics direct and legible. Persistence uses **SQLAlchemy** as an object-relational mapper over **SQLite** in development, with the code written to remain **PostgreSQL-ready**: using the ORM and avoiding engine-specific SQL means the database engine is a deployment choice rather than an architectural commitment.

The security-relevant libraries were chosen for their standing. Cryptographic hashing uses the standard library's **`hashlib`** for SHA-256, incurring no extra dependency; digital signatures and symmetric encryption use the **`cryptography`** library, which provides audited implementations of Ed25519, AES-256-GCM, and the Fernet token format used to protect private keys at rest. Authentication and session management use **Flask-Login**, cross-site-request-forgery protection uses **Flask-WTF**, rate limiting uses **Flask-Limiter**, and time-based one-time-password two-factor authentication uses **PyOTP** with **qrcode** for enrolment. File handling uses **Pillow** for EXIF metadata and **python-magic** for magic-byte content-type validation. The analytical and assistive features use **scikit-learn** for its Isolation Forest, a large language model consumed through the **NVIDIA NIM** OpenAI-compatible endpoint, and **ReportLab** for PDF generation. The presentation layer is server-rendered **Jinja2** templates styled with **Bootstrap 5**, a deliberately modest choice that keeps the interface functional and the scope realistic. Testing uses **pytest** with **pytest-cov**. The following table summarises the stack and the role each element plays.

| Layer | Technology | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Language & framework | Python 3, Flask | Application core, application-factory pattern, blueprints |
| Persistence | SQLAlchemy ORM, SQLite (dev) / PostgreSQL-ready | Relational data model, engine-independent queries |
| Authentication & session | Flask-Login, Flask-WTF (CSRF), Flask-Limiter, PyOTP + qrcode | Login, CSRF protection, rate limiting, TOTP 2FA |
| Cryptography | hashlib (SHA-256), cryptography (Ed25519, AES-256-GCM, Fernet, X.509/TLS) | Integrity hashing, signatures, encryption at rest, HTTPS |
| File metadata & validation | Pillow (EXIF), python-magic (MIME) | Metadata extraction, content-type validation |
| Machine learning & AI | scikit-learn (Isolation Forest), NVIDIA NIM (Llama 3.1) | Anomaly detection, natural-language assistant |
| Reporting | ReportLab | Court-style PDF chain-of-custody reports |
| Presentation | Jinja2, Bootstrap 5 | Server-rendered, role-tailored UI |
| Testing | pytest, pytest-cov | Automated tests and coverage measurement |

### 4.2 Application Architecture (Factory and Blueprints)

The application is structured around the Flask **application-factory** pattern: rather than creating a global application object, a `create_app()` function constructs and configures the application on demand. This is not an incidental choice — it is what makes the system testable, because each test can construct a fresh application bound to an in-memory database in isolation, and it avoids the circular-import problems that a global application object invites. The factory binds four extensions to the application — the SQLAlchemy database, the Flask-Login manager, the CSRF protection, and the rate limiter — then registers the route blueprints, creates any missing database tables, and runs a lightweight idempotent schema-migration step before returning the configured application.

Functionality is divided into ten **blueprints**, each grouping the routes for one area of the system: authentication, cases, evidence, custody, the dashboard, the audit trail, risk and anomaly views, the AI assistant, administration, and export. This separation keeps each area's routes cohesive and independently comprehensible. Cross-cutting behaviour that must apply to every request is implemented as request hooks on the application rather than repeated in each blueprint: a `before_request` hook enforces the sliding idle-session timeout, and an `after_request` hook attaches the security response headers described in Section 4.5.

Beneath the routes sits a distinct **business-logic layer** in the `app/logic` package, which is where the character of the system lives. This layer holds the cryptographic helpers (`crypto`, `file_crypto`, `keyderive`), input validation (`validators`), the four algorithmic engines (`graph_integrity`, `state_machine`, `risk_scoring`, `chain_reconstruction`), the anomaly-detection pipeline (`anomaly_detection`), the formula checks (`audit_formulas`), the AI assistant orchestration (`ai_assistant`), and the PDF report generator (`pdf_report`). Keeping this logic out of the route handlers means the algorithms can be unit-tested directly, without the machinery of an HTTP request, and it enforces a clean separation between *what the system computes* and *how it is exposed over the web*. The data model itself lives in a third package, `app/models`, with one module per entity. A small shared `utils` module provides a single source of truth for local-time handling, a detail that matters because inconsistent timestamp handling was a real source of defects until it was centralised.

### 4.4 Data Model and Entity-Relationship Design

The heart of the data-oriented layer is a relational model of six entities, shown in full in the entity-relationship diagram (Figure 4.3). The **Investigator** entity represents a user account, holding the credentials, the assigned role, the Ed25519 public key and the Fernet-encrypted private key, and the account-security fields (failed-login count, lockout timestamp, and TOTP secret) added during hardening. The **Case** entity represents an investigation, with its case number, title, jurisdiction, status, and the identifiers of its creator and assignee. The **Evidence Item** entity is the central object of the system: it carries its evidence number, its parent case, its category and lifecycle state, the file's name and stored path, the original and current SHA-256 hashes, the extracted metadata, the uploader and current holder, the computed risk score and level, and — added in the maintenance work — the latest integrity status that feeds the risk scorer and the soft-delete flag that lets an item be removed from listings while its history is preserved. The **Custody Log** entity records each custody event with its type, the investigators it moves between, its timestamp and location, the file hash at that moment, and the Ed25519 signature that makes it non-repudiable. The **Audit Record** entity captures every significant system event with its actor, the evidence and case it concerns, the originating IP address, the outcome, and the timestamp. Finally, the **Case Access** entity is the join between investigators and cases that implements case-level authorisation, recording the permission each member holds and who granted it.

The relationships among these entities encode the domain rules. A case has many evidence items and many access records; an evidence item has many custody-log entries and many audit records; an investigator uploads, holds, and transfers evidence and appears throughout the custody and audit histories. Two design decisions in this model deserve emphasis. The first is that the **audit trail is append-only**: updates and deletes on audit records are blocked at the ORM layer, so the history of what happened cannot be rewritten through the application. The second is that **deletion is soft**: removing an evidence item sets a flag that hides it from every listing while leaving its custody log and audit trail intact, because in a chain-of-custody system the history of an item — including the fact that someone deleted it — is itself potential evidence and must never be destroyed.

### 4.5 Security Architecture Overview

Security in DEICMS is not a feature bolted on at the end but a property distributed through the architecture, and it is treated in depth in Part VI; this section gives the overview needed to read the increment chapters. Security operates at several layers. At the **transport** layer, the application serves only over HTTPS and sends an HTTP Strict-Transport-Security header so that a browser, once it has connected securely, will not silently fall back to plain HTTP. At the **session** layer, authentication is managed by Flask-Login with a sliding idle timeout, optional TOTP two-factor authentication, and lockout after repeated failed logins. At the **request** layer, cross-site-request-forgery protection is applied to every state-changing form, the login route is rate-limited, and a Content-Security-Policy together with `X-Content-Type-Options`, `X-Frame-Options`, and `Referrer-Policy` headers provides defence-in-depth against cross-site scripting and clickjacking. At the **authorisation** layer, every route enforces a minimum role, and every access to case data is checked against the requesting investigator's case membership — a check applied not only to direct record access but, following the maintenance iteration, to every aggregate view and to the AI assistant's data retrieval. At the **data** layer, evidence files are encrypted at rest with AES-256-GCM, private keys are encrypted with a Fernet key derived from the application secret, uploads are validated by magic bytes so that a renamed executable cannot masquerade as an image, and custody events are signed with Ed25519 so that they cannot be forged or repudiated. At the **integrity** layer, the graph verifier can prove, cryptographically, that an item's entire custody history is intact. This layered arrangement means that the compromise of any single control does not by itself defeat the system, which is the defining characteristic of a defence-in-depth design and the appropriate posture for software whose entire purpose is to be trustworthy.
